# Change Station model and limits

The Change Station follows one representative 0.20 kg sample of water. The sample is divided among solid ice, liquid water, and water vapour. All three reservoir masses sum to 0.20 kg throughout the model. Eighteen indexed H₂O molecules remain in the particle view: only their arrangement and displayed phase assignment change. A molecule never turns into a different substance.

Melting, freezing, and boiling use the verified equilibrium model in `src/science/matterEngine.ts`. Energy is relative to ice at 0 °C; specific heat and latent heat values, including ideal plateaux at 0 °C and 100 °C, are documented in [SCIENCE_MODEL.md](./SCIENCE_MODEL.md). Heating and cooling add or remove 10 kJ per action. Energy detail is visible beside the controls; students can use the direct Heat and Cool actions without relying on the unfamiliar unit.

Vaporisation has two distinct modes. **Evaporation** begins with liquid water at 24 °C and transfers from its exposed surface below boiling. **Boiling** begins at 92 °C and follows the thermal engine through the 100 °C plateau, with vapour forming within the liquid. The USGS explains that water evaporates at temperatures below boiling and much more readily at its boiling point ([Evaporation and the Water Cycle](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/evaporation-and-water-cycle)). No white smoke is used to represent water vapour.

Condensation, sublimation, and deposition use a separate qualitative surface-transfer model. The surface is held at its selected temperature by an external thermal reservoir. Nearby air temperature is fixed for each scenario; relative humidity is an adjustable boundary condition. The model estimates ambient vapour pressure as relative humidity times a simplified saturation-vapour-pressure estimate for that air temperature. It compares that with the saturation pressure at the sample surface: liquid-surface pressure for evaporation/condensation and ice-surface pressure for sublimation/deposition. A qualitative flux follows `J = k × (p_surface − p_air)` for evaporation and sublimation, and `J = k × (p_air − p_surface)` for condensation and deposition. The sign sets the transfer direction and the magnitude sets the rate. At balance, there is no net transfer. A dry boundary can drive ice toward vapour; moist air at a suitable cold surface can drive vapour toward frost. Sublimation and deposition can occur under ordinary atmospheric pressure; this model does not require a low-pressure chamber.

The pressure curves use compact Magnus-style saturation-vapour-pressure approximations over liquid water and ice, clipped to a classroom-scale temperature range. Their use here only gives temperature and humidity controls a plausible causal gradient. Separate empirical calibration, wind, local boundary layers, radiative and latent heating, nucleation, and measured surface-area effects are omitted. Transfer multipliers are intentionally accelerated to make phase changes visible during a lesson; rates are illustrative and must not be read as measured predictions.

The environmental boundary conditions remain fixed while the tracked sample moves between its three reservoirs. The ledger is a deliberately closed 0.20 kg teaching sample, not an open atmospheric water-budget model: the ambient humidity condition does not create or remove sample mass. Flux is capped by available source mass. Surface-temperature limits preserve the selected condensed phase: evaporation remains at or below 60 °C, a condensation surface stays at or above 0 °C, and sublimation/deposition surfaces stay at or below 0 °C. An environment change may reverse the signed flux, and the scene reports the actual direction. A direct solid-to-gas or gas-to-solid route has zero liquid mass at every step.

The scene is an explanatory apparatus, not molecular dynamics or a scale drawing. The local 3D model now uses a graduated glass beaker with a visible rim and liquid surface above a controlled hot plate with a power indicator and heat/cool coil cues. These details make the physical setup recognisable; their dimensions and graduations are illustrative and do not feed the energy calculation. Ice is represented by a lattice-like cluster, liquid molecules exchange neighbours, and gas molecules move through the available headspace. During boiling, vapour molecules appear within the liquid bulk as a schematic bubble cue; no white smoke is used to represent everyday water vapour. Condensation droplets and deposition frost appear on the outside of the cold vessel, and only when the corresponding reservoir contains water. Drag or touch-drag to orbit the apparatus and scroll or pinch to zoom; the view control returns the camera to its starting angle. The 2D recovery view and the ledger use the same engine state. Turning particle view off hides the H₂O models but leaves the reservoir amounts, conditions, and controls available.

## Particle arrangement and hot plate (visual model)

- Ice: 18 molecules on fixed sites of an open hexagonal ring lattice (`iceLatticeSite`, linked neighbours drawn as pale links); they only vibrate about their sites.
- Liquid: packed about as closely as ice, scrambled over a loose grid and drifting collectively (`liquidDrift`), never a fixed pattern.
- Gas: spread through the whole beaker, moving fast in straight lines with wall bounces (`gasPosition`); `relaxSeparation` stops molecules overlapping.
- The hot plate coil glows orange only for ~1.8 s after Heat (or H) is used, glows blue after Cool (C), and is neutral grey otherwise. The scene exposes `data-heater` and a "Hot plate on/off" chip. The camera is fitted to the aspect ratio so the plate and beaker are never cropped.
